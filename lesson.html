Try STEM Bridge AI in one class period (45 minutes)

A ready lesson for a teacher who wants to see what students do with the tool and what the feedback looks like. One tool, one class, no setup beyond the link. It is built so the student does the thinking and the AI gives hints, not answers.

At a glance

Who it is for

Students in grades 2 to 12 for Science Writing, grades 6 to 12 for Coding Helper. Kindergarten and grade 1 can speak their answer where the browser supports it.

What you need

A device with a web browser for each student or pair, the site address, and one piece of student work to improve (a lab report, a hypothesis, or a few lines of code).

What students do

Paste their own work, press Check, read one next step, revise, and check again.

What it will not do

It will not rewrite the work or give the answer. It points to what to fix and suggests one thing to try.

Before class (about 10 minutes)

  1. Open the site yourself and press Try an example, then the blue Check button. You will see what a student sees. Press See a sample result if you want an example without waiting for the AI.
  2. Choose one tool. For a first try, one is enough (see the table below).
  3. Prepare a short piece of student-style work for the model part, such as a few sentences from your last lab, written by you.
  4. Read "Privacy at a glance" (the Privacy link at the bottom of the site). Students type only a first name, and their writing is sent to an AI service to get the feedback; it is not saved by this app.
  5. Do not use real student photos yet unless your school has approved the AI key. Photo Check needs that care; the text tools carry less risk.

The lesson, minute by minute

  1. 0–5minutes

    Welcome

    Open the site on the board. Show the three ideas on the first screen: try it yourself first, check the AI, make it yours.

    “Today the AI is a helper, not a crutch. It will not write for you. It will tell you one thing to try, and you decide what to change.”
  2. 5–10minutes

    Model it together

    Paste your short sample (or press Try an example) and check it on the board. Read the next step aloud. Ask: “Is this advice true for this text? How could we check?” Then show the Check this yourself box.

  3. 10–25minutes

    Students try it

    Each student (or pair) types a first name, opens the chosen tool, pastes their own work, and presses Check. They read the feedback and underline the one next step in their notes. Walk around and ask, “What is it asking you to do?”

    If students use a class code, they join from Have a class code?; this is optional.

  4. 25–35minutes

    Revise and check again

    Students change their work using the next step and press Check again. For a revision of the same work, a panel shows what changed since the last check. Small score changes are shown as “about the same”, because the AI’s scores move a little even on the same text.

    “A score is not the goal. Did the advice make your work clearer? Show your partner one change.”
  5. 35–42minutes

    Check yourself

    Pick two students to share one thing the AI said that they verified in a textbook or notes, and one thing they decided not to use and why. This is the most important part: it keeps the human in charge.

  6. 42–45minutes

    Exit

    Students write one sentence: “Today the AI helped me ___, and I still had to ___.” If you want, press Save as Word to keep a copy of the feedback.

If something goes wrong

What to know before you use it

This tool is new. It has been built and tested by its developer and has not yet had a classroom pilot, so there is no data yet on learning outcomes. The AI can make mistakes, and the feedback rubric has not been validated against teacher scoring. Use your own judgment, and do not use the scores for grades.

After class: tell us how it went

The most useful thing you can do is tell the project what worked and what did not. In the site, choose Rate this tool in the top bar. The form takes about two minutes.
